How to scout an opponent in football: a one-hour video method

You don't need a scouting department to prepare for next Saturday. With one recent match of your opponent and an hour of focused watching, you can find the two or three things that will actually change your game plan.

21 November 20265 min readCiprio Verhille

Why scouting is worth an hour

Most amateur and academy teams prepare for every opponent the same way. That is not always wrong: working on your own game is the priority. But one or two details about the next opponent can change a lot. Who takes their goal kicks short, which full-back never tracks back, which corner routine they use every time. Players remember these details because they are concrete, and they make the game plan feel specific to the match.

The trap is the opposite one: spending an evening watching a full match from start to finish and coming out with twenty observations nobody can use. Scouting is only useful if it ends with a short list.

Finding footage

At professional level this part is easy. At amateur level, it is often the hardest step. A few places to look:

  • Your own archive. If you played them earlier in the season, your own footage is the best starting point, even if both teams have changed since.
  • Their footage. Many clubs now film their matches and post full games or highlights on their website or social media.
  • Other coaches. A team that played them recently may be willing to share their video, especially if you can return the favour.
  • Going to watch. If there is no video at all, watching one of their matches live with a notebook and the same checklist still works.

One recent match is usually enough to start. Two is better if you can find them, because it tells you what is a habit and what was a one-off.

The one-hour method

Don't watch the match in order hoping something stands out. Watch it with questions, one phase at a time.

  1. Their shape (10 minutes). Watch the first few minutes and a few restarts. Note how they line up with the ball and without it. Do they press high or wait in a mid or low block? Who steps out?
  2. Their build-up (15 minutes). Jump to their goal kicks and to moments when their goalkeeper or centre-backs have the ball. Do they play short or long? Towards which side? Which player do they look for when they're under pressure?
  3. Their transitions (15 minutes). Find the moments they lose and win the ball. When they lose it, do they counter-press or drop? When they win it, how quickly do they go forward, and through whom?
  4. Their set pieces (10 minutes). Corners and wide free kicks, for and against. Set pieces are where teams repeat themselves the most, and where preparation pays off quickly.
  5. Individuals (10 minutes). Two or three players at most: the one everything goes through, the quickest attacker, and any defender who looks uncomfortable in a specific situation.

Write as you go, with the match time next to each note. You will need those timestamps to show the clips to your players.

From observations to a short list

After an hour you will have more notes than you can use. Keep only what passes two filters:

  • Did it happen several times? One bad pass from their centre-back is an accident. The same pass going wrong four times is a pattern.
  • Can your team do something about it? "Their winger is very fast" is true but not useful on its own. "Their winger always wants the ball to feet, so our full-back can stay tight" is something you can work on.

Aim for three points at most: one about how to attack them, one about how to defend against them, and one about set pieces. Everything else stays in your notebook.

Turning it into instructions

A scouting point only matters if it changes what someone does on the pitch. Turn each one into an instruction with a trigger, an action and a zone, as we described in our article on individual instructions you can measure. "Their centre-backs struggle under pressure" becomes "on their goal kicks, our two strikers stand on their centre-backs and force the goalkeeper to go long".

Then show it. Two or three short clips of the opponent doing the thing you've spotted are worth more than a long explanation. Players believe what they see.

Don't over-prepare

Opponents change: a different line-up, an injured key player, a new coach, a plan made specifically for you. Present your scouting as likely tendencies, not certainties, and keep your own principles at the centre of the plan. If your players spend the week thinking only about the opponent, they can lose what makes your own team work.

After the match, check what actually happened. Was the opponent like the footage? Did your instructions work? That quick review makes your next scouting report better, because you learn which kinds of observations are reliable and which aren't.
