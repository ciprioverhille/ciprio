How to run a football video debrief your players will actually learn from

Analysing the match is half the job. The other half is showing it to your players in a way that changes what they do next Saturday. Here is how to prepare a short, focused video debrief.

7 November 20265 min readCiprio Verhille

Why most video sessions don't work

Anyone who has played in a team that used video knows the scene: the whole squad in a room, the lights off, the coach scrolling through the match, stopping every two minutes. After a quarter of an hour, half the players are looking at their phones and the other half are waiting for their own mistakes to appear on screen.

The problem is rarely the footage. It is the preparation. A debrief that tries to cover the whole match covers nothing. Players leave with a vague feeling that "it wasn't good enough", but no idea of what to do differently.

A good debrief does the opposite: it picks a few situations, explains them clearly, and connects them to the next training session.

1. Start from the game plan, not from the match

Before you open the video, write down the two or three instructions you gave before the match. "Press their centre-backs when they receive facing their own goal." "Our full-backs never both go forward at the same time." "Switch the play when their block shifts to one side."

These are your themes. Every clip you show should answer one question: did we apply this instruction, and what happened when we did or didn't? If a clip does not relate to a theme, it probably belongs in an individual conversation, not in the team session.

Starting from the plan also protects you from a common trap: building the session around the goals conceded. Goals are memorable, but they are often the end of a chain that started much earlier, or simply bad luck. The patterns that repeat over the match usually matter more.

2. Choose few clips, and choose them well

Keep the team session short. Players' attention drops quickly when they are watching passively, so aim for a debrief that fits in about fifteen minutes, with a handful of clips per theme. A few rules help with the selection:

  • Show what worked as well as what didn't. A clip where the instruction was applied well shows the team what "good" looks like. It is often more useful than a clip of the mistake.
  • Prefer repeated situations. One mistake can be an accident. The same situation going wrong four times is a pattern worth a team discussion.
  • Start the clip a few seconds early. The cause of a problem is usually before the moment you want to show: a position, a body shape, a missing call.
  • Avoid singling out one player in front of the group. If one name keeps coming back, take those clips to an individual meeting instead.

3. Structure the session

A simple structure keeps the debrief focused:

  1. The reminder. Put the instruction on screen, in the exact words you used before the match.
  2. The positive example. One clip where it worked. Pause at the key moment and ask the players what they see.
  3. The problem. Two or three clips where it didn't. Again, ask before you explain.
  4. The fix. One sentence on what changes, and how you will work on it this week.

Asking questions instead of commenting every clip changes the room. Players who explain a situation themselves remember it much better than players who are told what they did wrong. It also tells you whether they understood the instruction in the first place, which is sometimes the real problem.

4. Make the clips easy to read

Footage that is clear for the coach who filmed it is not always clear for a player seeing it once on a screen. A few small things help:

  • Freeze the image at the decisive moment and draw on it if your software allows: an arrow for the run, a circle around the free space.
  • Replay the key clip twice: once at normal speed, once paused at the decision.
  • Give every clip a short title tied to the theme, for example "Switch of play: 23rd minute".

5. Connect it to training

The debrief is only useful if it changes what happens on the pitch. The best moment for it is early in the week, before the first session, so that the exercise you planned is the direct follow-up of what the players have just seen. If the theme was the switch of play, the first exercise of the week should reward switching the play.

Then close the loop: after the next match, check the same instruction again. The players will notice that what you show them on Tuesday is what you check on Saturday, and the debrief stops being a punishment and becomes part of the week.

A note on young players

With academy players, keep it even shorter, show more positive examples, and focus on one theme. The goal is to teach them to read situations, not to review every action of the match.
