How to give football players individual instructions you can actually measure

"Be more aggressive." "Stay switched on." Players hear these before every match, and nobody can say afterwards whether they did it. Here is how to turn individual instructions into something a player can apply and you can check.

14 November 20265 min readCiprio Verhille

The problem with vague instructions

Most individual instructions are given in the dressing room, a few minutes before kick-off, with the best intentions. "Win your duels." "Give us width." "Don't switch off." They sound clear. In reality, each player understands them a little differently, and after the match nobody can say for sure whether they were followed.

That has two consequences. The player gets feedback based on an impression ("you were a bit quiet today") rather than on facts. And the coach loses the chance to learn whether the instruction was a good idea in the first place. If you can't tell whether something was applied, you can't tell whether it worked.

What makes an instruction measurable

A measurable instruction answers three questions:

  • When? The trigger: the situation in which the player has to act. "When their left-back has the ball", "when we lose the ball in their half", "on our goal kicks".
  • What? One visible action. Not an attitude, a behaviour you could point at on a frozen image: press, drop, overlap, stay wide, mark the near post.
  • Where? A zone or a reference on the pitch: "inside the width of the box", "no deeper than the halfway line", "on the touchline".

"Be more aggressive" becomes: "When their number 6 receives with his back to goal, close him down from behind before he can turn." Now the player knows exactly when to act, and anyone watching the video can count how many times the situation happened and how many times he did it.

Examples by position

Some typical rewrites, to adapt to your own game plan:

  • Centre-back. Instead of "be dominant", try: "When their striker drops to receive, follow him up to the halfway line; the other centre-back covers."
  • Full-back. Instead of "help the attack", try: "When the ball is on the opposite flank, stay level with the last line; only overlap when our winger has the ball facing forward."
  • Defensive midfielder. Instead of "protect the defence", try: "When we lose the ball, your first move is to close the central lane in front of our centre-backs, not to press the ball."
  • Winger. Instead of "give us width", try: "When we build from the back on your side, start on the touchline, not inside."
  • Striker. Instead of "work hard", try: "On their goal kicks, stand between their two centre-backs and force the kick long."

None of these is a universal rule. They only make sense inside a given plan against a given opponent. The point is the shape of the sentence, not its content.

Fewer instructions, written down

A player who receives five individual instructions on top of the team ones will remember one or two, usually the last ones he heard. Give each player one instruction, two at most, and make sure it connects to the team plan rather than adding a separate layer.

Write them down. A short card, a message the day before, or a board in the dressing room all work. Writing forces you to be precise, and it gives you the exact wording to check against after the match. It is also fairer to the player: he is judged on what you actually asked, not on what you remember asking.

Before the match, ask the player to repeat the instruction in his own words. If he can't, the problem isn't his attention, it's the instruction.

How to check it on video

Once the instruction has a trigger, an action and a zone, checking it becomes a counting exercise:

  1. Find every occurrence of the trigger. Every time their number 6 received with his back to goal, every goal kick, every loss of the ball in their half.
  2. For each one, note whether the action happened. Yes, no, or "couldn't": sometimes the situation made the instruction impossible, and that is useful information too.
  3. Look at the result. When the player did it, what followed? When he didn't, what followed?

You end up with something simple and honest, like "the situation happened twelve times, you did it eight times". That is a conversation starter, not a verdict. Show the player two or three of the clips, including one where he did it well.

Separate respect from result

An instruction can be perfectly applied and still not work, because the opponent adapted or because the idea was wrong. It can also be ignored and the team still wins the duel. Keep the two questions apart:

  • Did the player do what was asked? That is about him.
  • Did it produce what we wanted? That is about the plan, and so about you.

Mixing the two is how players end up blamed for a coach's tactical choice, or praised for a good outcome that had nothing to do with the instruction.

Over the season

When individual instructions are written the same way every week, they start to build a record. You can see whether a young full-back is getting better at choosing when to overlap, or whether a striker keeps forgetting the pressing trigger in the second half. That is far more useful in an end-of-season review than a general impression.
